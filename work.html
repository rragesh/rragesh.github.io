Robotics projects, explained.

From a robot finding its charger to a rover finding its way across rough ground. Every project starts with the idea, then explains the engineering.

Autonomous docking: finding the way back to charge

A robot that can patrol on its own also needs a way to return to its charger. This project built and tested that return-to-charge routine in simulation.

Ingeniarius · Coimbra, Portugal

Helping robots find their place indoors

Before robots can work together, they need to agree on where they are. This project used overhead infrared cameras to give them a shared view of an indoor workspace.

École Centrale de Nantes · France

SAKSHA: teaching a robot by moving an arm

What if showing a robot a movement were as direct as moving an arm yourself? SAKSHA explored that idea with two linked robotic arms and a record-and-replay workflow.

Amrita University · India

ASTRA: giving robots a shared view

A robot on the ground and a robot in the air see different parts of the same place. ASTRA explored how those perspectives could be connected in a multi-robot system.

Amrita University · India

DHRUV: a closer look at hazardous spaces

Before entering a potentially dangerous area, people need information. DHRUV brought environmental sensing, cameras, and a robotic arm together on a remotely operated rover.

Amrita University · India

Phoenix II: one rover, fifteen people

A planetary rover brings many engineering disciplines into one machine. I led a 15-person team building Phoenix II for the 2016 United Kingdom Rover Challenge.

United Kingdom Rover Challenge · Manchester · 2016

Phoenix I: bringing a rover team’s work together

I led the Phoenix I project for the 2016 University Rover Challenge in Utah. It was an early lesson in turning a team’s individual contributions into one working robot.

An eight-wheeled rover with a different way over obstacles

A wheel can only climb so far before the vehicle’s shape gets in the way. This student rover explored what happens when some wheels can also be lifted on leg-like supports.

Questions about working together.

When is it a good time to work together?

When your robots work in pilots but releases, customer-site deployments, or field incidents are becoming harder to manage. The assessment is a starting point for teams preparing to expand a fleet or improve an existing deployment workflow.

Do you implement changes or only advise?

Both are possible. An assessment produces findings and an implementation roadmap. A separately scoped implementation engagement can cover release pipelines, OTA workflows, observability, and team handover. Fractional leadership provides ongoing technical direction with an agreed scope and level of involvement.

Can you work with our existing team and infrastructure?

Yes. The starting point is your current robot platform, release process, and team. My experience covers ROS and ROS 2, on-premise, hybrid, and cloud-connected environments. We agree the access and engineering involvement needed for the scope before starting.

How are scope, timing, and fees agreed?

Start with a LinkedIn message describing your fleet, deployment bottleneck, and next milestone. We discuss fit, then agree deliverables, responsibilities, timing, and fees before work begins. Implementation and ongoing support are scoped explicitly.
