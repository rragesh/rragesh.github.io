From planetary rovers
to the software factory.

I’m Ragesh. I started by building robots that could move, sense, and act. Today, I build the infrastructure that helps teams release, update, and operate those robots reliably.

The thread through my work is the same: connect engineering to what actually happens on the machine, in the field, and in the team responsible for it. Here’s how that perspective developed.

First, make the robot work.

2013–2017 · Amrita University & rover challenges

My foundation was electronics and communication engineering at Amrita University. Robotics brought the pieces together: sensors, embedded systems, control, software, and the mechanical reality underneath them.

In 2015, I worked on system integration for a planetary rover. In 2016, I led a 15-person multidisciplinary team building a rover with autonomous navigation and manipulation capabilities for the United Kingdom Rover Challenge. Phoenix I and Phoenix II became milestones in learning how a complete robot comes together.

That work gave me an enduring systems perspective. A robot is not a collection of independently successful components; it has to work as one machine, with a team able to diagnose what happens when it does not.

At the Mechatronics and Intelligent Systems Research Lab in 2017, I developed EMG-based control for a prosthetic arm, working on real-time signal processing and adaptive actuation.

What I carry forward: deployment decisions need to respect hardware, timing, and integration constraints—not just the software build.

Then, understand how it fails.

2017–2019 · École Centrale de Nantes & Fraunhofer IPA

A master’s in Control and Robotics at École Centrale de Nantes deepened my understanding of how robots perceive and act. My work included multi-camera infrared localization, ROS integration, and calibration.

For my master’s thesis at Fraunhofer IPA in 2019, I developed fault detection for industrial manipulators and automated planning to generate recovery actions after abnormal operating conditions.

Other early work included autonomous docking for service robots at Ingeniarius, training through RobotCraft, robotic teaching arms, and coordinated ground-and-air robot systems. I also founded Phyction Technology Labs during my broader robotics journey.

What I carry forward: a working system needs a recovery path. Detection, diagnosis, and recovery belong in the design from the start.

From research systems to factory floors.

2019–2023 · Fraunhofer IPA / ROS-Industrial

At Fraunhofer IPA, I developed and deployed ROS 2-based autonomous robot fleets in industrial environments. The work spanned architecture, system integration, and field operation.

I translated project and industry-partner requirements into technical designs for EU-funded robotics projects. Alongside the robot software, I built CI/CD and deployment workflows to make delivery more repeatable.

Through the ROS-Industrial Consortium, I delivered ROS 2 training and technical guidance to industry partners. Explaining a system to the people who will extend and operate it became part of the engineering work.

What I carry forward: a solution has to fit the customer’s environment and be understood by the team that owns it. Architecture, deployment, and handover are connected.

Make delivery a product capability.

2023–2025 · Building Robotair

I founded Robotair to connect software repositories to configurable delivery workflows, over-the-air updates, and fleet observability. The aim was to bring cloud-like software delivery workflows to robotics teams.

During a 12-month beta, the platform reached 15 paying beta customers across Europe and India. Building it connected the infrastructure problem to another question: can a team adopt this capability and find enough value in it to pay for it?

I later applied the platform’s technical approach and domain experience to in-house software delivery capabilities at NODE Robotics. These chapters overlapped; the work in one informed the other.

What I carry forward: infrastructure has to earn its place through adoption, usability, and commercial value—not simply technical sophistication.

Make it repeatable across a production fleet.

2024–2025 · NODE Robotics

At NODE Robotics, I owned the software delivery infrastructure end to end and scaled it to support more than 1,500 production robots across different customer environments.

  • Deployment on demand. Delivery infrastructure enabled customer deployments on demand while supporting three software releases per month.
  • Repeatable provisioning. Ansible and Terraform workflows standardized provisioning and deployment across fleet and backend systems.
  • Lower infrastructure cost. A hybrid on-premises and VPC architecture reduced infrastructure costs by 50%.
  • Visible delivery performance. DORA metrics and deployment KPIs gave engineering stakeholders visibility into release performance and system stability.

What I carry forward: scale needs more than automation. It needs repeatable environments, operational visibility, and architecture that makes economic sense.
